# ADR-0031: Claude's answers are streamed, and a scene plan's room follows its narration

- Status: Accepted
- Date: 2026-11-25
- Issue: #267
- Amends: ADR-0029 (decision 9: a careful request no longer has a wait of its own)
- Builds on: ADR-0030 (a paid call is sent once, and a lost answer is recorded), which it applies to a streamed answer
- Amended by: ADR-0034 (a stopped job gives up its request)

## Context

Every request to Claude was sent with one token limit and one clock on the whole answer: 16,000 tokens and three minutes, or 32,000 tokens and ten minutes for a careful request (ADR-0029). The scene plan is the one answer whose size follows the video's length: a few lines once (the story, the cast, the places) and then an image prompt and a motion prompt for every scene.

To see how large it gets, a plan was written under the default template for a narration of 12 sentences and counted with Anthropic's public tokenizer, which is the one of its earlier models; the current models count the same text as up to about a third more, and the ranges below run from the one to the other. No request was sent. A scene takes 120 to 165 tokens, a cast member about 100, and the story and the places about 230 together. Before any thinking, the plan of a one-minute video is 1,100 to 2,700 tokens, of a ten-minute video 9,000 to 25,000, of a fifteen-minute video 14,000 to 37,000. The thought before the answer is billed and counted against the same limit; how much a plan takes was not measured.

So Shorts and three-minute videos fit either limit. The plan of the long format (8 to 10 minutes) passes 16,000 tokens in the upper half of its range, and with 32,000 it fits only while the thought stays small; a fifteen-minute plan passes 32,000 on its own at the top of its range. An answer cut at the token limit is billed in full and nothing of it is kept, and the job could then be started again by hand at the same price.

Two limits are the provider's and do not move: a model writes at most 128,000 tokens in one answer, thinking included, and a request that is not streamed is expected to finish within ten minutes.

## Decision

1. **Every Claude request is streamed.** The adapter sends `stream: true` and reads the server-sent events as they arrive: the text, the model that wrote it (the fallback's, when a declined request moved to it mid-answer), the reason it stopped and the usage. When more than one model worked on the answer, the usage is the sum of what each attempt used (`usage.iterations`), recorded under the model that answered. A body that is one JSON message (a server that does not stream) is still read; which of the two a body is, is told by how it begins, not by its headers.
2. **The limit is on silence, not on the answer.** A connection that says nothing for ten minutes is given up on, and one that keeps talking is left alone however long the answer takes. Claude sends keep-alives while it thinks, but how often is not documented, and the two mistakes do not cost the same: waiting on a dead connection costs minutes, and giving up on a live one costs the whole answer, which is billed. Ten minutes is also what a careful request waited before, so a path that holds an answer back until it is whole (a proxy that inspects it) delivers what it delivered before. Finding the server and connecting to it have 30 seconds each. A bound of two hours on the whole answer remains only as a last resort.
3. **A stream that stops says where, and only a request that started nothing is sent again.** The rules of ADR-0030 hold for a streamed request, read at the point a stream adds: after the status and before the answer.
   - The request goes again, after a wait, only when Claude says nothing was started: a 429, a 503 or 529 in the API's own error report, or an `error` event of overload or rate limit in the place of the answer (as the first event of a stream, or as the whole body of a successful status). It never goes again when the answer's `x-should-retry` header says `false`, which the API asks clients to follow; a server error with that header is a lost answer, so the queue does not send it either.
   - Any other error that opens the stream is read as its type says, like the same error as a status; a request the API calls invalid there is handled as a 400 is. A 500 in the API's own report is an outage the queue retries. Any other 5xx comes from something in front of the API and is a lost answer.
   - `timeout_error` is the exception among the API's own reports: it means the request reached the server's time limit while Claude worked on it, so it is a lost answer whatever status or place it comes in.
   - Once anything of an answer arrived and the stream then stops (the connection drops, goes silent, or an `error` event ends it), or a successful status brings nothing that is an answer (nothing readable, or a message that says nothing and has no stop reason), the failure is a lost answer (`AnswerLost`): Claude took the request, and what it wrote is billed whether or not it arrived. The adapter does not send it again and the queue does not retry it; a second attempt is the user's choice.
   - A request that got no status is unreachable when it never left this computer and a lost answer otherwise, as the transport tells them apart.
   - An answer that gave its stop reason is whole even if the stream ends before its last event; content after a stop reason means the answer was not over. An answer cut at the token limit or at the context window, or one without text, carries its usage, streamed or not. A refusal carries it when Claude bills it: when something was written before it, or when its category is one of those the API bills before any output (`bio`, `frontier_llm`, `reasoning_extraction` as of September 2026). The API reports the usage of the others too and does not bill them.
   - A lost answer carries no usage. A stream counts its output only at its end: before that it has said the input and a placeholder, and recording those would leave out everything Claude wrote. Costs records what the call usually uses instead (ADR-0030, decision 6); for a scene plan that is the size of point 6, not the mean of other plans.
4. **A request can say how large its answer gets.** `TextRequest` gains `answer_tokens`. With it the adapter asks for four times that room (the thought before the answer, and an answer that runs longer than it was sized), never less than the request's usual limit and never more than the model's 128,000. Without it nothing changes. Room that is not used is not billed.
5. **The plan's size comes from the narration.** `ScenePlanSize` gives the scenes a narration can have at most (one every four seconds, never more than its sentences) and usually (one every six seconds), and from them the tokens of the answer at most (2,500 + 250 a scene) and usually (600 + 160 a scene). The plan request sends the first as `answer_tokens`. These are padded figures, not hard limits: a template the user edited, or long aesthetic notes repeated in every prompt, can make a scene larger, which is what the room of point 4 is for.
6. **The estimate is the size, not the history.** Before planning, the call is priced at the usual answer plus its thought (10,000 tokens, or half the answer when that is more), on the Scenes stage and in the whole-video estimate alike (the input is the prompt's length where the prompt is known, which is on the Scenes stage). Other calls are estimated from the mean of the last calls of their kind; for the plan that mean mixes videos of every length, so one long plan would make every Short look expensive.
7. **A narration that asks for too much is refused before it costs.** When the plan's room at most passes half of what the model can write (about 245 scenes: a little over 16 minutes of short sentences, more when they are long), **Plan scenes** is not offered and the stage says why. The job checks again, so a plan queued another way fails without asking Claude. The other half is the margin: such a plan might fit, but one that does not is paid for and lost.
8. **Planning in parts is left out.** Asking for the cast and the places first and then the scenes in runs of sentences would lift the limit of point 7, but each part is its own paid call, the parts must agree with each other, and a failure midway needs saved checkpoints so the earlier parts are not paid twice. Bardo's own formats end at ten minutes, well inside one answer.
9. **The per-request wait of ADR-0029 goes.** No request has a clock on its whole answer, so `HttpRequest` no longer carries one. The streaming transport keeps no idle connections, as every paid call's does (ADR-0030, decision 4).

## Consequences

- The plan of a long video arrives: about a hundred scenes take a few minutes, during which the job runs as any other.
- A request's cost does not change. Streaming is the same call at the same price, and the larger limit is only room.
- The estimate before planning a long video is higher than before, because it was too low. The share assumed for thought is a guess until plans of every length have been paid for.
- A narration longer than about 16 minutes cannot be planned in one project, though some such plans would fit. If long narrations become something people make, planning in parts (point 8) is the change.
- After a silence, the abandoned connection and the thread that reads it stay until the two-hour bound: the HTTP client offers no way to close them sooner. On a path that holds a long answer back for more than ten minutes, whether it holds the status back too or only the body, the request fails once as a lost answer and Claude may still write, and bill, the whole answer.
- Cancelling a plan job did not stop its request when this was decided: the answer was still written and billed, as before, and the job now runs for minutes. ADR-0034 stops the stream when its job stops.
- A plan that is lost midway is recorded in Costs at the plan's estimate. That can be wrong in both directions, as every lost answer's record can (ADR-0030): a stream that stopped early wrote less, and one that thought for long wrote more.
- When a fallback model took part in an answer, the usage recorded is the most the answer can cost, not always the bill: an attempt that declined before it wrote anything is billed only for some reasons, and each attempt is billed at its own model's rate. Recording each attempt under its own model would need more than one cost record per call.
- The whole-video estimate still prices the plan of a narration too long to plan; the Scenes stage is where it is refused.
- The stream's event shapes are the provider's documented ones, and the adapter's fixtures are written from them, not recorded. A long plan against the live API, and the longest silence inside it, are checks of the regression pass, not something this change confirmed.
